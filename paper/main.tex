\title{Where Two-Stage Cover Song Retrieval Loses Its Covers:\\
Candidate Recall, Hubness and Alignment Cost on Da-TACOS}
\author{Anonymous submission}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Cover song identification (CSI) must recognise a composition across changes of key, tempo, instrumentation and structure.
Structure-aware alignment handles these changes well but costs quadratic time per pair, so scalable systems shortlist $K$ candidates with a learned embedding and align only those.
Alignment cannot recover a cover that the shortlist excludes.
We measure this constraint on all 13{,}000 queries of the Da-TACOS benchmark with a transparent HPCP system: a compact temporal convolutional encoder, then key-rotated subsequence dynamic time warping over the shortlist.
Every setting was locked on held-out works before the benchmark was run.
Because Hit@1 factorises exactly into shortlist coverage and reranker efficiency, we can assign every query's failure to one stage.
At the deployed $K=30$, \MissThirty\% of queries have no cover in the shortlist.
Across four locked evaluations, coverage accounts for about \CoverageShareOfGain\% of the log-gain in a \HitOneGain$\times$ improvement of Hit@1. The gap to exhaustive 96-frame alignment narrows from \ClassicalOverHybOne$\times$ to \GapPctFour\% in MAP, with 30 instead of 14{,}999 alignments per query.
Enlarging the shortlist raises MAP from \SweepMapThirty{} to \SweepMapFiveHundred{} at $K=500$, but shifts the dominant failure from exclusion to misranking between $K=100$ and $K=200$.
Hub correction matters more as $K$ grows: without it, a tenth of the catalogue supplies \POneUncFiveHundredTopDecile\% of the rank-1 false positives in covered queries at $K=500$.
Two registered interventions on the coarse stage give opposite results. Per-query adaptive $K$ does not beat fixed $K$ at equal cost. Fusing global and window embeddings raises MAP at every $K$ (\ATwoApThirty{} at $K=30$).
Finally, in four paired comparisons a 20-query development protocol was inconclusive every time and pointed the wrong way three times.
Our absolute accuracy is well below published systems. The contribution is the measurement of where a two-stage system fails, not a new model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A cover preserves a composition's identity while changing almost everything else: key, tempo, instrumentation, timbre, and often structure.
Classical CSI handles these changes explicitly. It compares chroma sequences after choosing a transposition and aligns them in time \citep{serra2008,serra2009qmax}.
Alignment is accurate but costs $O(NM)$ per pair, and a catalogue of $n$ tracks needs $n^2$ pairs. On the Da-TACOS benchmark \citep{datacos} that is \TotalPairs$\times10^8$ query--candidate pairs.
Learned CSI replaces alignment with a single embedding per recording, compared by cosine similarity \citep{move2020,bytecover,discogsvi}. It is fast but blurs temporal structure.

The natural compromise is \emph{coarse-to-fine} retrieval. An embedding shortlists $K$ candidates per query, and structure-aware alignment reranks only those. ByteCover3 retrieves with approximate nearest neighbours and reranks with local features \citep{bytecover3}; LIVI reranks with late interaction over local embeddings \citep{livi2026}; text retrieval has used cascades for over a decade \citep{wang2011cascade}.
The design has an obvious ceiling: a cover the coarse stage excludes cannot be recovered.
What is not obvious is \emph{how large} that ceiling is at realistic shortlist sizes, how much of a system's improvement comes from raising it, and when it stops being the main limit.
We found no CSI study that reports these quantities.

We measure them on the full Da-TACOS benchmark (13{,}000 queries, 15{,}000 candidates, 12 relevant items per query). The system is deliberately transparent: HPCP features, a \Params-parameter temporal convolutional encoder, exact cosine search, key rotation by chroma-profile cosine, and slope-constrained subsequence DTW.
Every hyperparameter was locked on held-out works before the benchmark was run.
The system is far from state-of-the-art accuracy. What it offers is complete control: exact per-query ranks for every stage, four locked benchmark evaluations, a sweep of $K$ from 5 to 500 that reproduces the locked run exactly at $K=30$, and paired, work-level statistics.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A per-query decomposition of two-stage CSI.} Hit@1 factorises exactly into shortlist coverage and reranker efficiency. We use this to attribute a \HitOneGain$\times$ Hit@1 improvement mostly to the coarse stage, and to locate the shortlist size ($K\approx100$--$200$) where misranking overtakes exclusion as the main failure (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:failures}).
\item \textbf{A controlled study of what moves each stage}: training data and training length, window fusion in Stage 1, alignment resolution, key handling, and hubness correction, whose benefit grows with $K$. It includes a registered negative result for per-query adaptive $K$ (Sections~\ref{sec:ablations}--\ref{sec:failures}).
\item \textbf{An accuracy--computation analysis} that separates what is measured from what was never run (Section~\ref{sec:compute}).
\item \textbf{A paired demonstration that a 20-query development protocol cannot substitute for benchmark evaluation} in this setting, with an explanation in terms of protocol structure (Section~\ref{sec:devbench}).
\end{enumerate}
We do not claim a new architecture, a new hubness correction, or state-of-the-art accuracy. Two-stage retrieval, local features and hubness correction are all prior art (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Classical CSI.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Harmonic pitch class profiles (HPCP) \citep{gomez2006} are the pitch-class features Da-TACOS distributes.
\item Serr\`a et al.\ choose an optimal transposition index and align binarised chroma locally \citep{serra2008}; Qmax quantifies cross-recurrence \citep{serra2009qmax}.
\item Our Stage 2 uses the same ingredients in simpler form: profile-based key choice and whole-query subsequence DTW \citep{sakoe1978,muller2015}.
\item Scalability has been a concern since at least \citet{bertinmahieux2012}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Learned version identification.}
\begin{itemize}
\item MOVE and Re-MOVE learn embeddings from pitch-class input \citep{move2020,remove2020}.
\item ByteCover and ByteCover2 train CQT encoders with multiple losses \citep{bytecover,bytecover2}. CoverHunter adds attention and coarse-to-fine alignment during training \citep{coverhunter}.
\item Discogs-VI provides 1.9 million versions and an open model \citep{discogsvi}. CLEWS learns segment-level representations \citep{clews}.
\item Our encoder combines a generic temporal convolutional network \citep{bai2018tcn} with a supervised contrastive loss \citep{khosla2020supcon}. Neither component is new.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Two-stage and local retrieval.}
ByteCover3 uses local features and a two-stage pipeline of ANN retrieval and re-ranking \citep{bytecover3}. LIVI shortlists with track embeddings and re-ranks with MaxSim over local embeddings \citep{livi2026}, following late interaction in text retrieval \citep{colbert}.
Multi-stage IR studies the cost--effectiveness trade-off of cascades \citep{wang2011cascade}, predicts per-query candidate depth \citep{culpepper2016}, and reports that rerankers degrade as they score more candidates \citep{jacob2024drowning}.
Our work sits between these lines. We do not propose a stage; we measure what the interface between the stages costs in CSI.

\paragraph{Hubness.}
Some items are near neighbours of disproportionately many queries. This is known in audio similarity \citep{aucouturier2008} and in high-dimensional spaces generally \citep{radovanovic2010,schnitzer2012}.
Corrections that subtract a candidate's typical similarity include CSLS \citep{csls} and querybank normalisation \citep{qbnorm}. In CSI, hubness has been reduced with mutual proximity \citep{li2018}, at feature summarisation \citep{hu2019}, and by normalising alignment similarity with a hubness score \citep{seo2022}.
Our correction is of this family.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
Da-TACOS \citep{datacos} distributes features, not audio, and defines the 15{,}000-track benchmark used here.
Recent work moves to much larger and noisier collections: Discogs-VI \citep{discogsvi}; DiVers, with more than a million versions including in-the-wild YouTube audio \citep{divers}; studies of robustness to real-world covers \citep{hachmeier2025}; and joint track and version identification benchmarks \citep{araz2026unified}.
MIREX CSI results use private collections and are not comparable with Da-TACOS numbers, so we do not quote them.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the candidate set ($|\mathcal{C}|=15{,}000$). For query $q$, let $\mathcal{R}_q\subset\mathcal{C}\setminus\{q\}$ be its relevant set ($|\mathcal{R}_q|=12$). Stage 1 induces a ranking $\pi^{(1)}_q$ of $\mathcal{C}\setminus\{q\}$. Let
\[
r_q=\min_{c\in\mathcal{R}_q}\pi^{(1)}_q(c)
\]
be the Stage-1 rank of the first relevant item.

A $K$-candidate reranker permutes the first $K$ items of $\pi^{(1)}_q$ and leaves the rest unchanged.

\paragraph{Coverage and efficiency.} Query $q$ is \emph{covered} at $K$ iff $r_q\le K$. Define
\begin{align*}
C(K) &= \Pr_q[r_q\le K] &&\text{(coverage)},\\
\eta(K) &= \Pr_q[\text{rank-1 item is relevant}\mid r_q\le K] &&\text{(reranker efficiency)},\\
\mathrm{Hit@1}(K) &= C(K)\,\eta(K) &&\text{(exact).}
\end{align*}
The identity holds because an uncovered query cannot have a relevant item at rank 1.
Shortlist recall is $\mathrm{Rec}(K)=\mathbb{E}_q\,|\{c\in\mathcal{R}_q:\pi^{(1)}_q(c)\le K\}|/|\mathcal{R}_q|$. Coverage asks for at least one cover in the shortlist; recall asks for all 12.

\paragraph{Failure classes.} Every query at a given $K$ falls in exactly one class:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A}: not covered;
\item \textbf{B}: covered, but a non-cover is at rank 1;
\item \textbf{R}: a cover is at rank 1.
\end{itemize}
Within B we mark \textbf{B}$_\text{hub}$ when the rank-1 item's hub reference (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is above the 95th percentile of the catalogue, a threshold fixed before the analysis.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the system; Table~\ref{tab:t14_config} lists every setting, read from configuration and result files.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{architecture.pdf}
\caption{The two-stage system with run-4 settings and measured costs. Stage 1 scores every candidate; Stage 2 aligns only the $K$-item shortlist (\ShortlistPct\% of candidates at $K=30$).}\label{fig:arch}\end{figure}

\paragraph{Features.} Each recording is a Da-TACOS HPCP sequence $X\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{12\times T}$. For alignment it is frame-normalised, area-resampled to $N\in\{96,384\}$ frames, and frame-normalised again. The encoder view is resampled to 256 frames.

\paragraph{Stage 1: global embedding.} A non-causal temporal convolutional network \citep{bai2018tcn} maps $X$ to a unit vector $e(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$. It has six residual blocks with kernel 3 and dilations $1,\dots,32$ (receptive field \ReceptiveField{} of 256 frames), then mean and max pooling over time and an MLP head.

The encoder is trained with a supervised contrastive loss \citep{khosla2020supcon} (temperature 0.1) on batches of 32 works $\times$ 2 recordings. Augmentation is random time crops (60--100\%) and random cyclic pitch rotation. Optimisation is AdamW with cosine learning-rate decay; the checkpoint with the best validation MAP is kept.

Stage 1 scores $g(q,c)=e(X_q)^\top e(X_c)$ exactly over all candidates and keeps the top $K$.

\paragraph{Key handling.} Let $\bar p(X)$ be the $\ell_2$-normalised time-average of $X$, and $\rho_k$ cyclic rotation of the pitch axis by $k$ semitones. We choose
\[
k^*(q,c)=\arg\max_{k\in\{0,\dots,11\}}\bar p(X_q)^\top\rho_k\,\bar p(X_c)
\]
in the spirit of the optimal transposition index \citep{serra2008}, and align $X_q$ with $\rho_{k^*}X_c$.

\paragraph{Subsequence DTW.} With frame-wise cosine cost $C_{ij}=1-\cos(x^q_i,(\rho_{k^*}x^c)_j)$, the accumulated cost is
\[
D_{i,j}=\min_{(a,b)\in\Sigma}\;D_{i-a,\,j-b}+w_{ab}\,C_{ij},\qquad \Sigma=\{(1,1),(2,1),(1,2)\},\quad w=(1,2,1),
\]
with $D_{0,j}=C_{0,j}$ for all $j$, so the query may start anywhere in the candidate. The path ends at $\arg\min_j D_{N-1,j}$.

The step set bounds the path slope between $1/2$ and $2$, which rules out degenerate paths that match the whole query to one candidate frame. With these weights every admissible path has weighted length $N$, since each query frame is paid for once. Dividing the accumulated cost by the path weight therefore gives a normalised cost $\bar D$ whose minimiser is the same path.

The alignment score is $a(q,c)=1-\bar D(q,c)$.

\paragraph{Fusion.} Within each shortlist, the scores are z-normalised and blended:
\[
h(q,c)=\alpha\,z_q(g)+(1-\alpha)\,z_q(a).
\]
Candidates below the shortlist keep their Stage-1 order.

\paragraph{Hubness correction.} A candidate that aligns well with everything gets a penalty:
\[
a'(q,c)=a(q,c)-\lambda\,\bar a(c),\qquad \bar a(c)=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}a(p,c).
\]
Here $P$ is a fixed set of 200 probe queries from the \emph{training} split, so no test query is involved. This is the candidate-side term of CSLS \citep{csls}; the query-side term is constant within a query's ranking and is omitted. Its cost is $200\times15{,}000$ extra alignments, done once.

\paragraph{Locked settings.} $\alpha$ is chosen by grid search in steps of 0.05, $\lambda$ from a grid, and the alignment resolution $N$ is fixed, all on ten \emph{calibration} works. Checkpoints are chosen on 150 validation works.

\section{Experimental Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Data and roles}
Da-TACOS \citep{datacos} provides HPCP features, not audio. Features and metadata are licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, and nothing derived is redistributed here. Table~\ref{tab:t1_protocol} gives the work-disjoint roles:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{training}: 4{,}780 works, or the first 1{,}500 of them for the smallest encoder;
\item \emph{validation}: 150 works, used for checkpoint selection;
\item \emph{calibration}: 10 works, used for $\alpha$, $\lambda$ and $N$ only;
\item \emph{development}: 20 query works, 40 distractor works;
\item \emph{benchmark}: the separate Da-TACOS benchmark subset.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Benchmark}
All 13{,}000 clique recordings (1{,}000 works $\times$ 13) are queries against all 15{,}000 tracks, which include 2{,}000 noise tracks, with self-matches excluded. Each query therefore has 12 relevant items among 14{,}999 candidates.
The Cover Analysis and benchmark subsets share no work and no recording (0 common WIDs, 0 common PIDs; \texttt{DATASET\_CARD.md}), so training cannot leak into the benchmark. The benchmark was used only for final evaluation. No setting was chosen on it, and no locked run was repeated after its result was seen.

\subsection{Development protocol}
\DevQueries{} queries each rank a pool of \DevCandidates{} candidates containing exactly one relevant item, so AP equals reciprocal rank. The protocol was built as a fast plumbing check. Section~\ref{sec:devbench} analyses what it can and cannot measure.

\input{tables/t1_protocol.tex}

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
We report MAP, MRR, Hit@$k$, first-relevant rank, coverage $C(K)$ and shortlist recall.

Intervals are 95\% paired bootstrap intervals with 10{,}000 resamples. We resample \emph{query works} (1{,}000 cliques), not queries, because the 13 recordings of a work share relevance sets and are not independent \citep{efron1993,field2007}.
We call a difference supported only when its interval excludes zero. Intervals are per comparison and not adjusted for multiplicity; most supported benchmark differences exclude zero by a wide margin. Development intervals resample the 20 query works and are correspondingly wide.

\subsection{Evidence tiers}
Each result is labelled with how it was obtained:
\begin{itemize}
\item \tier{frozen}: the four locked benchmark runs, with committed per-query files;
\item \tier{dev}: the development protocol;
\item \tier{analysis}: computed only from committed per-query files;
\item \tier{local-full}: later runs over all 13{,}000 queries on the author's laptop, validated before use. At $K=30$ they reproduce run 4 (match: \ReproPass; $|\Delta|=0$ for all four systems' MAP and for shortlist recall);
\item \tier{post-hoc}: an analysis defined after its inputs were seen.
\end{itemize}
Runs A2 and E1 were registered, with success criteria, before their results existed.

\subsection{Shortlist size: accuracy keeps rising, and the bottleneck moves}
The frozen runs fix $K=30$. Run A1 aligns the top 500 candidates of every query once and derives every $K\le500$ from that pass (\tier{local-full}). Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep} and Table~\ref{tab:t4_k_sweep} give the results.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Accuracy.} Hub-corrected MAP rises from \SweepMapFive{} at $K=5$ to \SweepMapThirty{} at $K=30$, \SweepMapHundred{} at $K=100$ and \SweepMapFiveHundred{} at $K=500$. Hit@1 rises from \SweepHitFive\% to \SweepHitFiveHundred\%. Quality has not saturated at $K=500$.
\item \textbf{The bottleneck moves} (Figure~\ref{fig:failbyk}, Table~\ref{tab:t8_failure_by_K}). Class A shrinks from \ClassAFive\% of queries to \ClassAFiveHundred\%, while class B grows from \ClassBFive\% to \ClassBFiveHundred\%. They are close at $K=100$ (\ClassAHundred\% vs.\ \ClassBHundred\%), and B dominates from $K=200$ (\ClassBTwoHundred\% vs.\ \ClassATwoHundred\%).
\item \textbf{Efficiency falls.} Reranker efficiency drops monotonically from \EffFive\% to \EffFiveHundred\%. Each additional candidate is one more chance to recover a cover and one more chance for a non-cover to outrank it.
\end{itemize}
Text rerankers show the same diminishing returns with depth \citep{jacob2024drowning}; here it is measured for alignment reranking in CSI.

The claim that coverage is the dominant limitation therefore holds only for $K\lesssim100$ in this system.

\section{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablations}
Table~\ref{tab:t3_ablations} gives paired benchmark ablations from the frozen runs; Table~\ref{tab:t11_alignment_ablation} gives the alignment ablation at $K=30$ (H1).

\paragraph{Training data and length.} Training on 4{,}780 instead of 1{,}500 works raises validation MAP from \ValSmall{} to \ValFull{}. Training for 150 instead of 60 epochs raises it to \ValLong{} (best epoch \ValLongBestEpoch).
The 150-epoch curve rose between epochs 91--120 and 121--150 (means \ValMeanNinetyOne{} and \ValMeanOneTwentyOne), then flattened: the fitted slope over the last 30 epochs is \ValLongSlope{} per epoch, as the cosine schedule decays the learning rate to zero. The data establish neither convergence nor continued improvement.
In Stage-1 coverage at $K=30$ (F1), the 60-epoch encoder is \FOneSixtyThirty{} points below the 150-epoch one and the 1{,}500-work encoder \FOneSmallThirty{} points below. Training scale is the largest single effect we measured on the coarse stage.

\paragraph{Alignment resolution.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \tier{dev}: 384 frames raise classical MAP from \DevClassicalLow{} to \DevClassicalHigh{} and hybrid MAP from \DevHybLow{} to \DevHybHigh.
\item Benchmark at $K=30$ (run 3 vs.\ run 2): the effect is smaller but supported, \ResGain{} [\ResGainLo, \ResGainHi] AP (Table~\ref{tab:t3_ablations}).
\item H1, rerank only at $K=30$: 96 frames cost \HOneShortAp{} [\HOneShortApLo, \HOneShortApHi] relative to 384.
\end{itemize}
Temporal area-pooling to 96 frames averages away harmonic changes that alignment could otherwise match. The development result for \emph{exhaustive} alignment at 384 frames has no benchmark counterpart.

\paragraph{Key handling (H1).} Removing the key rotation costs \HOneNoRotAp{} [\HOneNoRotApLo, \HOneNoRotApHi] AP and \HOneNoRotHit{} points of Hit@1. Trying all 12 rotations instead of the profile choice adds only \HOneExhAp{} [\HOneExhApLo, \HOneExhApHi] at twelve times the alignment cost.

\paragraph{Hubness correction.} Hub correction improves every system (Table~\ref{tab:t3_ablations}).
\begin{itemize}
\item It raises exhaustive alignment from \ClassicalMap{} to \ClassicalHubMap.
\item At $K=30$ it converts \HubFixedShare\% [\HubFixedLo, \HubFixedHi] of class-B queries into successes, while breaking \HubBroken\% of all queries.
\item Its MAP gain grows with $K$: \HubGainFive{} at $K=5$, \HubGainThirty{} at $K=30$, \HubGainFiveHundred{} at $K=500$.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:failures} explains why.

\paragraph{Changing Stage 1 without retraining (F1, A2).}
\begin{itemize}
\item Test-time key rotation of the query adds \FOneTtrThirty{} [\FOneTtrThirtyLo, \FOneTtrThirtyHi] points of coverage at $K=30$.
\item Maximum similarity over three overlapping 60\%-length windows \emph{loses} \FOneWinMaxThirty{} points on its own.
\item Averaging that window score with the global cosine adds \FOneFuseThirty{} [\FOneFuseThirtyLo, \FOneFuseThirtyHi] points. The window count, lengths and the 50/50 weight were fixed in advance.
\end{itemize}
A registered end-to-end test of the fused Stage 1 (A2; criterion: paired $\Delta$MAP above zero at $K=30$ and $K=100$) is \ATwoMeets.
MAP rises at \ATwoAllKPositive{} $K$ we measured: \ATwoApThirty{} [\ATwoApThirtyLo, \ATwoApThirtyHi] at $K=30$ and \ATwoApHundred{} [\ATwoApHundredLo, \ATwoApHundredHi] at $K=100$. Hit@1 rises \ATwoHitDThirty{} points at $K=30$.
The gain is largest at small $K$, where coverage is the bottleneck. With fusion, $K=\ATwoMatchK$ matches the MAP of the global Stage 1 at $K=30$ (Figure~\ref{fig:a2}, Table~\ref{tab:t13_a2_end_to_end}).
Late interaction is not new \citep{colbert,livi2026}; the result shows that its coverage gain survives alignment reranking.

\paragraph{Allocating $K$ per query (D1, E1).} Label-free Stage-1 score statistics predict whether a query will be uncovered at $K=30$, with cross-fitted AUROC \DOneAuroc{} [\DOneAurocLo, \DOneAurocHi].
A registered three-tier policy that sets $K$ from this prediction, at equal mean cost, meets its criterion in \EOneMeets{} of \EOneCells{} settings. Wherever it raised MAP it lowered Hit@1, so it is a trade-off, not an improvement (Appendix, Table~\ref{tab:t7_adaptive_k}). Adaptive candidate depth is established in IR \citep{culpepper2016}; it did not transfer here.

\section{Failure Analysis}\label{sec:failures}
\paragraph{Classes at scale.} Section~\ref{sec:results} and Table~\ref{tab:t8_failure_by_K} give the exact decomposition over all 13{,}000 queries. The hub subclass B$_\text{hub}$ is small with correction at every $K$, but it grows without correction (last column of Table~\ref{tab:t8_failure_by_K}).

\paragraph{Why hubs appear: static candidates.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mechanism.} A recording whose chroma barely changes over time has a nearly constant cost row against any query. Subsequence DTW then finds a cheap path through it regardless of the query's content.
\item \textbf{Development pool} (120 candidates): tonal dispersion correlates with how often a candidate is a top-10 false positive, with Spearman \RhoClassical{} for classical alignment and \RhoHybrid{} for the hybrid. The worst hub appears in \WorstHubCount{} of 20 top-10 lists, against \UniformHubCount{} expected by chance.
\item \textbf{Probe reference.} The reference $\bar a(c)$ correlates with dispersion at \RhoProbeLow{} (96 frames) and \RhoProbeHigh{} (384 frames). Subtracting it therefore targets exactly the static candidates.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Hubs at benchmark scale (P1).} Figure~\ref{fig:hub} asks where the rank-1 non-covers of covered-but-misranked queries sit in the catalogue-wide distribution of $\bar a$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Uncorrected:} the top decile of candidates supplies \POneUncThirtyTopDecile\% of these false positives at $K=30$ and \POneUncFiveHundredTopDecile\% at $K=500$, against 10\% under uniformity.
\item \textbf{Corrected:} the shares fall to \POneCorThirtyTopDecile\% and \POneCorFiveHundredTopDecile\%.
\item \textbf{Why the value grows with $K$:} a larger shortlist admits more hubs, which is why the correction's value grows with $K$.
\item \textbf{The two stages have different hubs:} the Stage-1 $k$-occurrence of a candidate is nearly unrelated to its alignment reference (Spearman \POneKoccRho). Correcting the embedding stage was also not selected on calibration ($\lambda=\FOneHubLambda$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Case studies (\tier{dev}).}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Alignment rescue} (Figure~\ref{fig:case_success}): for query P\_797406, Stage 1 ranks the true partner 15th. Alignment finds a clean diagonal path in the same key and moves it to rank 1.
\item \textbf{Shortlist exclusion} (Figure~\ref{fig:case_miss}): query P\_310762's partner is ranked 48th by Stage 1. Exhaustive alignment would rank it 11th, but it never reaches the $K=30$ shortlist.
\item \textbf{Harmful reranking}: query P\_207372's partner falls from Stage-1 rank 5 to hybrid rank 22.
\end{itemize}
These cases illustrate the three classes. They are chosen examples, not estimates of frequency.

\item \textbf{Reranking at $K=30$:} \RerankSecondsLow{} s at 96 frames (\RerankMsLow{} ms per query) and \RerankSecondsHigh{} s at 384 frames (\RerankMsHigh{} ms per query). It touches 390{,}000 of \TotalPairs$\times10^8$ pairs (\ShortlistPct\%).
\item \textbf{Exhaustive alignment} at 96 frames took \ExhaustiveSeconds{} s (\ExhaustiveHours{} h, alignment only). The hub-corrected variant adds a one-off probe reference of \HubProbeSecondsLow{} s.
\item \textbf{Not run:} exhaustive alignment at 384 frames, estimated at about 42 h.
\end{itemize}

The hybrid curve crosses the 96-frame exhaustive system between $K=50$ and $K=100$ with the global Stage 1, and earlier with the fused one.

\end{itemize}
Three caveats limit this comparison: it was defined after seeing A1; the two systems use different resolutions (384 vs.\ 96 frames); and the timings come from different machines.
Neither faster nor more accurate settles the matter alone. The hybrid buys a large reduction in alignment work with a coverage risk that the decomposition makes explicit.

\section{Development-vs-Benchmark Protocol Analysis}\label{sec:devbench}
We asked the same paired question of both protocols four times (P2, Figure~\ref{fig:devbench}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item Hybrid minus exhaustive alignment, run-1 encoder:
  \tier{dev} \PTwoADev{} [\PTwoADevLo, \PTwoADevHi];
  benchmark \PTwoABench{} [\PTwoABenchLo, \PTwoABenchHi].
\item Reranking minus Stage 1, 150-epoch encoder:
  \tier{dev} \PTwoBDev{} [\PTwoBDevLo, \PTwoBDevHi];
  benchmark \PTwoBBench{} [\PTwoBBenchLo, \PTwoBBenchHi].
\item Hub correction of exhaustive alignment:
  \tier{dev} \PTwoCDev{} [\PTwoCDevLo, \PTwoCDevHi];
  benchmark \PTwoCBench{} [\PTwoCBenchLo, \PTwoCBenchHi].
\item Key rotation minus none:
  \tier{dev} \PTwoDDev{} [\PTwoDDevLo, \PTwoDDevHi];
  benchmark \PTwoDBench{} [\PTwoDBenchLo, \PTwoDBenchHi].
\end{enumerate}
The development interval included zero in \PTwoDevInconclusive{} of \PTwoN{} cases, and its point estimate had the wrong sign in \PTwoSignFlips. The benchmark interval excluded zero in \PTwoBenchDecisive{} of \PTwoN.
The development protocol did not ``lie''. It was inconclusive, and its point estimates were read as conclusions.

\paragraph{Why the protocols diverge.} Three structural differences explain it.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The shortlist covers different fractions of the pool.} $K=30$ is \DevShortlistShare\% of a \DevCandidates-item development pool but \ShortlistPct\% of the benchmark's 14{,}999. Development shortlist recall was \DevRecallMin--\DevRecallMax{}, against \BenchRecallMin--\BenchRecallMax{} on the benchmark (X6). The development protocol therefore measures the reranker given near-perfect coverage, a regime the benchmark never enters at $K=30$.
\item \textbf{One relevant item against twelve.} With one relevant item, AP equals reciprocal rank, so the metric rewards only the first cover. With twelve, MAP also rewards recovering the rest, which reranking a short list cannot do.
\item \textbf{Sample size.} 20 query works give intervals of $\pm$0.05--0.2 AP. The effects that matter at scale are 0.005--0.05.
\end{enumerate}
A useful development protocol for two-stage CSI must reproduce the benchmark's candidate-to-relevant ratio at the shortlist, and must be large enough for its intervals to exclude effects of that size.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What we show.}
\begin{itemize}
\item At $K=30$ the coarse stage is the main limitation, and improving it produced most of the observed gains.
\item The dominant failure moves from exclusion to misranking as $K$ grows, while accuracy keeps rising to $K=500$.
\item Hub correction transfers from calibration to 13{,}000 queries and matters more at large $K$, because hubs concentrate among high-reference candidates.
\item Fusing global and window embeddings improves the whole system. Per-query adaptive $K$ does not.
\item 384-frame alignment helps on the benchmark at $K=30$, by much less than on development.
\item A 20-query development protocol is inconclusive on every comparison we tested.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we suspect.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The location of the crossover ($K\approx100$--$200$) depends on the encoder and the reranker. A stronger Stage 1 should move it to smaller $K$.
\item Adaptive $K$ likely fails because the rank of the first cover is heavy-tailed even for queries predicted to be hard, so extra alignments are better spent on queries that are already covered.
\item Exhaustive alignment at 384 frames would probably widen its lead over the $K=30$ hybrid, as development suggests. It might not beat the hybrid at larger $K$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we did not test.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Exhaustive 384-frame alignment on the benchmark (not run; about 42 h estimated).
\item Any other dataset or feature type.
\item A strong external encoder. Da-TACOS has no audio, so audio models such as Discogs-VINet \citep{discogsvi} cannot be run on it. Pitch-class models such as MOVE and Re-MOVE \citep{move2020,remove2020} can, because Da-TACOS distributes their CREMA-PCP input.
\item Fusion settings other than the one registered.
\item Local (Qmax-style) alignment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Absolute accuracy.} Qmax reaches 0.333 MAP on the same HPCP input \citep{datacos}; our best locked system reaches \ClassicalHubMap, and the best measured configuration \ATwoMapFiveHundred. Findings about a weak system may not transfer to strong ones.
\item \textbf{Scope.} HPCP only; one dataset; features without audio; training works with two recordings each; a single global vector in the frozen Stage 1; whole-query rather than local alignment.
\item \textbf{The development protocol} is not representative of the benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:devbench}).
\item \textbf{Provenance.} The frozen runs record commits with uncommitted changes (``-dirty''). The local-full runs were made on a different machine, also from working trees with uncommitted changes, which is recorded in each run's environment file. A1 reproduces run 4 exactly, and cross-run equality checks pass, which bounds but does not remove this concern.
\item \textbf{Registration timing.} A2 was registered after its coverage effect (F1) was known, though before any end-to-end result. Only one fusion setting was evaluated.
\item \textbf{Reproduction boundaries.} Checkpoints are not redistributed, since derived weights inherit the non-commercial licence. They are reproducible bit for bit from the committed configuration.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future}
In order of what the evidence asks for:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Re-run the decomposition with a strong open encoder that accepts Da-TACOS features, such as MOVE or Re-MOVE on CREMA-PCP \citep{move2020,remove2020}, to test whether the crossover and hub findings generalise.
\item Choose fusion settings (window count, length, weight) on calibration works, and evaluate test-time key rotation end to end.
\item Improve discrimination inside large shortlists, where most failures are at $K\ge200$: stronger hub-aware scoring, local alignment.
\item Run exhaustive 384-frame alignment once, to close the gap in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}.
\item Build a development protocol whose candidate-to-relevant ratio matches the benchmark's.
\end{enumerate}
These are proposals. None has been run unless it is reported above.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
In coarse-to-fine cover retrieval, candidate selection is a measurable bottleneck, not an implementation detail.
On Da-TACOS, a transparent two-stage system is limited mainly by coverage at $K=30$, and improving the coarse stage produced most of its gains. Enlarging the shortlist keeps paying but hands the bottleneck to the reranker. Hub correction and a fused global--window first stage are among the few changes that help at every $K$.
The same analysis on a 20-query development set would have supported none of these conclusions.
